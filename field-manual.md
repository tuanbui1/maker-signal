## How to use this book

This is a field manual. Run it. Do not collect it.

1. Read **§0** once. That is the operating system.
2. Name which of the **four money objects** you are actually building (§1). Most operators are building two and pretending they are one.
3. Price the next file or offer with **§2** before you write a landing page.
4. Run the **paid-flip decision** (§3) before you pick a host.
5. Run the **kill system** (§4) on every live tab, every Sunday.

If a section asks you to invent a number we did not source, leave the cell blank. Empty is honest. A filled-in guess is how this manual would fail its own rules.

## 0. The one-page operating system

Strip the names off the 2026 cases and they collapse to five rules. Everything after this page is how to run them.

**1. Own a list.** A weekly newsletter in a sharp niche is still the connective tissue. beehiiv's first-party *State of Newsletters 2026* says publications launched in 2025 hit a median first dollar in 66 days, a median 482 subscribers in month one, and a median 8,314 by the end of year one. Weekly is the dominant cadence (46.67% of creators). The sister report, *State of Paid Newsletters 2026*, says the typical creator flipped on a paid tier 45 days after starting — a median of people who already charged, not a permission slip. Median free-to-paid conversion is 0.62%. The AI vertical leaks 13.33% of paid subscribers a month. Write the issues before you pick a host. Stay free until paid is a different product.

Sources: [The State of Newsletters 2026, beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026) · [The State of Paid Newsletters 2026, beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-paid-newsletters-2026)

**2. Ship a portfolio of related things, not a mythic flagship.** On 4 January 2026 Marc Lou published a self-reported "$1,032,000 in 2025." In the same post he said the year was down about 20%, mixed in $147,000 of index-fund returns and $14,339 of X payouts, and wrote that nine of ten TrustMRR verticals failed. Fernando Pessagno's 2026 Indie Hackers writeup is the un-glamorous twin: $500k+ lifetime (self-reported; header more than $15K a month) across three paid design tools and a free funnel, plus a graveyard of forks of the same problem. Hit-rate is the strategy. A portfolio of zombies is a support desk with extra domains.

Sources: [Marc Lou, "I made $1,032,000 in 2025"](https://newsletter.marclou.com/p/i-made-1-032-000-in-2025) · [Fernando Pessagno on Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/getting-fired-and-using-it-as-motivation-to-build-a-successful-niche-portfolio-nabmB9HOZvkx4J9902j6) · [Solopreneurship.eu case lab](https://solopreneurship.eu/articles/case-lab-marc-lou-1m-solo-year/)

**3. Price like a solution.** InsightRaider's June 2026 analysis of 146,271 Gumroad products says the $30–$49 band converts about 28% better than sub-$10 equivalents. Products under $10 are roughly 35% of listings and about 0.8% of platform revenue. 44% of products have generated exactly $0 in lifetime revenue. Tiered products with two or three tiers generate about 2x the revenue of single-price listings. Newsletter default price has not moved since 2024: $10 a month or $100 a year. Business sits at a median $15; investing at $27; travel at $7. Sub-$10 packs and hourly billing are how you work more for less.

Sources: [InsightRaider, How to Price Digital Products on Gumroad in 2026](https://insightraider.com/en/answers/how-to-price-digital-templates) · [beehiiv paid report](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-paid-newsletters-2026) · [Press Gazette, June 2026](https://pressgazette.co.uk/newsletters/newsletters-2026-prices-retention-churn/)

**4. Write the stop-loss before you fall in love.** Zero monthly recurring revenue is a message. Low-but-not-zero after a year of grinding is a drain. A self-reported $12,400 MRR can still be a guilt machine if churn is a treadmill and the number cannot pay the humans. Fast kills (six days, a pre-written $100 rule) and late kills (twelve months, a unit-economics rule that arrived at month nine) are the same skill on different calendars. Half-alive is not a state you are allowed to keep.

Sources: [Pessagno](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/getting-fired-and-using-it-as-motivation-to-build-a-successful-niche-portfolio-nabmB9HOZvkx4J9902j6) · [OperatorBook, killing a SaaS at $12K MRR](https://www.operatorbook.dev/stories/why-we-killed-our-saas-at-12k-mrr) · [Felix Lenhard, Kill or Commit, 20 January 2026](https://felixlenhard.com/blog/kill-or-commit-how-to-decide-on-struggling-projects/)

**5. Disclose first. Then distribute.** July 2026 is when the platforms stopped pretending generic AI was a growth hack. YouTube named three inauthentic buckets and took them out of the Partner Program. Substack handed every reader a scan button and every writer a "How I make this" box. Medium already refuses to paywall AI-generated text, disclosed or not. The Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report put trust in AI-chatbot news answers at 20% globally, against 37% for news overall. On 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act started requiring people to be told when they are talking to a machine. The remaining path for a cited, AI-disclosed brief is X plus an owned email list.

Sources: [TechCrunch, 20 July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/) · [The Verge, 21 July 2026](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/968855/substack-pangram-ai-detecting-tool) · [Medium AI content policy](https://help.medium.com/hc/en-us/articles/22576852947223-Artificial-Intelligence-AI-content-policy) · [Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2026](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary) · [Article 50, EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)

Checkout, domains, tax, and DMs are owner work. The asset is the issues, the file, and the kill-list. The host is a form.

## 1. Name the money object

Most stalled operators are running two businesses under one tab. Name the object. Price it. Kill the rest.

**Operator rule.** Pick one object as this quarter's *active* bet. The others may exist as drafts. Actively managing more than two or three live things at once is, in the Solopreneurship.eu "Projectologist vs Founder" essay, not realistic. The rest have to be assets on autopilot or they are jobs you are failing to quit.

Source: [Solopreneurship.eu, Projectologist vs Founder](https://solopreneurship.eu/articles/projectologist-vs-founder-portfolio-solo/)

**Sequence, not stream-count.** Brainchild360's June 2026 stack, citing the same beehiiv clocks, is the order we actually follow: consulting, then newsletter, then affiliates, then products, then membership. The newsletter is connective tissue, not the first checkout. Affiliates convert on warm trust, not cold posts. Kit's 2024 creator-economy survey, as cited there, found full-time creators averaging 6+ income streams and $100k earners averaging 7+; Lumanu/Quasa's 2025 survey put $150k earners at 7+ streams and low earners at two. Stream count is not the driver. Sequence is.

Source: [Brainchild360, Creator income stack, June 2026](https://www.brainchild360.com/creator-income/creator-income-stack/)

If you are an AI-assisted operator with no audience and no identity documents in the loop, the object you can finish this week is a file or a brief. The object you cannot finish is a live storefront or a service. That is not a vibe. It is the difference between an asset and a blocked owner step.

## 2. Pricing rules you can run without a storefront

Do not launch the $9 pack. Write the price before the landing page. Keep the listing off the live storefront until a legal person can run checkout.

### 2.1 Files and toolkits

InsightRaider's June 2026 cut of 146,271 public Gumroad listings is an estimate, not audited payouts. Treat it as a floor, not a promise.

| Rule | Number | What you do with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conversion band | $30–$49 converts ~28% better than sub-$10 | Default the next file into this band. This manual's standard price is $39. |
| Catalog trap | Under $10 = ~35% of listings and ~0.8% of revenue | If the only reason the price is $9 is "it will convert," you are in the band that is a third of the catalog and under one percent of the money. |
| Zero-revenue mass | 44% of products have generated exactly $0 lifetime | A listing is not a business. A long description and a real job-to-be-done are the filter. |
| Category averages | Software $39.95; ebooks $50.91; courses $95.74 | A playbook that is a system can sit with software. A reprint of blog posts should not pretend to be a course. |
| Tiers | Two or three tiers generate ~2x the revenue of a single price | Draft $39 / $69 / $129 (see the tier descriptions at the end). Do not invent a $9 "impulse" SKU to "complete the ladder." |
| Pay-what-you-want | Can lift unit sales ~8% and crush average order value unless you anchor it | If you use PWYW, the suggested price *is* the product. An un-anchored $0 floor is how you work for tips. |
| Take rate | Gumroad ~13.2% effective on a typical US sale (10% plus processing) | At $49 you keep roughly $42. At $9.99 you keep about $8.40 and need ~119 sales to net $1,000. |

Source: [InsightRaider](https://insightraider.com/en/answers/how-to-price-digital-templates)

**File-pricing checklist (no account required).**

1. Write the job in one sentence a buyer would pay to finish this week.
2. Put the price in the $30–$49 band, or write why this file is a course (closer to $95.74) and what delivery that implies.
3. Draft two more tiers that add *files*, not adjectives. "More support" is not a file if you cannot send a DM.
4. Write a long description (aim at 5,000 characters when a storefront exists). Feature lists do not sell a $39 object. The job does.
5. Keep it off the live storefront. Checkout, KYC, and bank payouts are owner steps. Lemon Squeezy and Paddle both charge 5% + $0.50 and remit VAT; Lemon Squeezy is faster to stand up, Paddle is deeper on complex subscriptions and enterprise invoices. That comparison is an owner decision, not a week-1 task.

Source for merchant-of-record: [Contra Collective, Paddle vs Lemon Squeezy, 2026](https://contracollective.com/blog/paddle-vs-lemon-squeezy-merchant-of-record-digital-commerce-2026)

### 2.3 Services

The 2026 freelancer migration is off hourly and onto a published offer: fixed scope, fixed price, repeatable delivery, often subscription-billed. Delivvo's 4 May 2026 guide cites Upwork's Future Workforce Index 2025 (roughly 28% of US knowledge workers freelancing independently; $1.5 trillion in 2024 earnings; $85,000 median full-time freelancer income), MBO Partners 2025 (5.6 million independents earning $100,000+), and Plutio's 2026 magazine numbers: value-priced freelancers at a $96,000 median versus $58,000 hourly. Designjoy is the canonical solo example they use — a published design subscription that reached a roughly $2 million a year run-rate by 2025. The same piece says 74% of independents now use generative AI and report saving about nine hours a week, which is exactly why hourly billing punishes you for getting faster.

Source: [Delivvo](https://delivvo.io/blog/productized-services-for-freelancers-2026)

A productized service is owner-gated for an AI operator. Contracts, invoices, and human communication need a legal person. The pattern is still worth stealing for later: one repeating deliverable, one number on a page, a delivery template, sell it ten times. Do not put a "book a call" button on a file that should have been $39.

## 3. The paid-flip decision

The 45-day number is now a slogan. It is also a median of people who already charged.

### 3.1 Two clocks, one mistake

Hold the two beehiiv clocks next to each other. First dollar around day 66 (*State of Newsletters 2026*). Typical paid flip around day 45 (*State of Paid Newsletters 2026*). The first dollar is often an ad, a boost, or a one-off — not a paid-subscriber business. The 45-day flip is when the median creator *turned the switch on*, not when the median creator had a product worth paying for.

Publishers on beehiiv sent 28 billion emails in 2025 and reached more than 255 million unique readers. Average open rate moved from 37.98% in 2024 to 41.24% in 2025. Paid subscription revenue on the platform hit $19 million in 2025, up 138% from $8 million in 2024. The share of revenue-generating users earning through subscriptions doubled from 15% in Q1 2024 to 30% in Q1 2026. Subscriptions went from roughly 30% of on-platform creator revenue to about 85% over the same window. The report projects $35 million by the end of 2026 — a projection, not a close. Emitpost's 10 July 2026 write-up quotes beehiiv CEO Tyler Denk saying the market is still early: most consumers do not pay for even one newsletter.

Sources: [beehiiv free report](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-newsletters-2026) · [beehiiv paid report](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-paid-newsletters-2026) · [Emitpost, 10 July 2026](https://emitpost.com/beehiiv-paid-newsletter-revenue-doubles-subscriptions/) · [Digital Applied, 5 July 2026](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/newsletter-statistics-2026-data-points)

The same paid report is explicit about who should ignore the clock. Creators who launch paid in week one tend to be people who already have an audience elsewhere and are migrating. Everyone starting from scratch is told to use the first six weeks to learn what readers reply to. Publishers who treat paid as "I'll just put some posts behind a paywall" get stuck near 0.3% conversion. Publishers who build paid as a separate product reach 5% or more. The long-term aim the report itself names is 2–5%, not the 0.62% median and not the 18–20% top-decile finance screenshots.

Sources: [beehiiv paid report](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/the-state-of-paid-newsletters-2026) · [Digital Content Next](https://digitalcontentnext.org/blog/2026/07/28/what-paid-newsletter-economics-reveal-about-subscriber-value/) · [Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/newsletters/newsletters-2026-prices-retention-churn/)

beehiiv states the compounding directly: 5% monthly churn means roughly 54% of a cohort is still around at month 12. 17% monthly churn means about 11% make it. That is a 5x difference in who is still paying a year later, before price enters the picture. The report calls the lifetime spread "roughly a 3x difference in revenue per subscriber before pricing is even factored in."

AI is the cautionary case. Press Gazette quotes the report's reasons: weaker initial purchase intent, a crowded and fast-moving space, and a value proposition that erodes as free AI content improves. "AI publishers need to stay ahead of the free content curve, or their paid tier becomes optional." An AI list at the $10 default is in the Community LTV neighborhood with Money-like leak — the worst of both tables.

Two retention moves in the same report are unglamorous and load-bearing.

- **Annual from day one.** Annual billing overtook monthly as the primary source of subscription revenue by mid-2025. At the start of 2025, monthly was about 70% of subscription revenue; by mid-year it was under half. The report's advice is to launch with annual at a 15–20% discount, not to bolt it on six months later. Digital Applied notes that several published verticals already sit closer to 5–14% off (investing's $292 against 12 × $27 is about 10%) and subscribers still chose annual. Commitment, not the size of the discount, is the gift.
- **Dunning and a cancel flow.** Automated retries plus a plain "your payment failed" email recover 20–40% of failed payments. A cancel flow with a survey, a pause, or a targeted offer can recover 10–20% of people who start to leave. If you do not have an onboarding plan for the first 48 hours after someone pays, the report's line is blunt: you are likely not ready to launch paid.

Preeya Goenka, beehiiv's chief customer officer, is quoted calling a paid subscription a product launch, not a paywall. If you cannot name the paid product in one sentence that does not include the words "the free issue, but more," you are not ready.

### 3.4 The other market is tired

The last verified Substack milestone we can stand on is 5 million paid subscriptions in March 2025. Simon Owens, writing on 8 May 2026 and citing Hollywood Reporter data from the same month, notes the company was still being described as "5 million-plus," with no confirmed 6 million mark more than a year later. Digital Applied's 5 July 2026 stats page refuses a circulating "8.4 million paid subscriptions in Q1 2026" claim as having no attributable primary. We are not using 8.4 million.

Sources: [Simon Owens, 8 May 2026](https://simonowens.substack.com/p/is-substacks-subscription-growth) · [Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/newsletter-statistics-2026-data-points)

Nieman Lab, on 1 April 2026, citing New York Times reporting, describes one reader paying about $600 a year for 11 newsletter subscriptions and another reporting $3,000 in annual newsletter costs. The same piece covers Trustfnd, a 2026 bundler that let three journalists sell a 30-day pass to all three titles for $8.50 — half the combined individual price. Native cross-publication bundling did not exist on beehiiv, Substack, or Patreon as of that article. A new paid tier is competing for wallet share, not arriving in an empty market.

Source: [Nieman Lab, 1 April 2026](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/04/three-newsletters-for-the-price-of-1-5-independent-journalists-experiment-with-a-bundle/)

Grey Journal puts ads next to subscriptions at small scale, using Paved CPM ranges it cites separately: a single $40 CPM placement on a 10,000-person list is $400; one a week is about $20,800 a year, against $7,440 from subscriptions at the 0.62% median. Ads can win *faster* at median conversion. Subscriptions overtake ads when conversion climbs toward 5%. The practical sequence is not "paid first." It is: own a list, learn what people will pay for, then layer.

Source: [Grey Journal](https://greyjournal.net/hustle/grow/how-much-do-newsletters-make/)

### 3.5 Do-not-flip-paid checklist

No account. No card. No DM. If you fail two or more, stay free.

1. Write `list × 0.0062 × monthly price × 12` on one line with your actual or honest projected list. If the number only works when you silently swap in 5%, you are using top-decile execution as a plan.
2. Look up your vertical in the price / conversion / churn / LTV tables before you look up a host. If a cell is unpublished, leave it blank.
3. Name the paid product in one sentence that does not describe the free issue. "The archive," "an extra section," or "the same brief without the free intro" is the 0.3% product. The report's test: exclusive data, community access, or a fundamentally different content depth.
4. If the topic is AI tools, prompts, or "what launched this week," treat 13.33% monthly churn as the default. That is roughly 7.5 months of lifetime and about $75 LTV at $10.
5. Draft the first 48 hours before you draft the paywall: welcome, what they get this week, what the free list does not get, where the archive is, what annual costs.
6. Price annual on the same page as monthly, from day one. $10 / $100 is the market default.
7. Do not flip in week one unless the audience already exists somewhere else.
8. Do not pick a host so you can charge. Write three issues first. beehiiv vs Substack vs Ghost is an owner step. Substack's 10% plus Stripe is a real cost at scale and a real distribution network at the start. beehiiv's 0% cut plus a flat fee wins later. None of that matters if the issues do not exist.
9. Count issues, replies, and flops — not the day number. Your number is: three issues shipped, two topics that got a reply, one sentence for the paid product, and a written onboarding sequence.
10. Stay free if paid would be a tax on the same brief.

Maker Signal's own paid object, if it ever exists: deeper notes, the source table, the kill-list of lanes that died — not a paywall on the free brief. Until that object is written, 45 is someone else's statistic.

## 4. The kill system

Zero is clarifying. A number that can pay the humans is a business. Everything in between is the guilt machine.

### 4.1 The feeling test and the $12K version

Pessagno's diagnostic is physical, not financial. Real traction has a feeling. You are not dragging a lifeless body uphill. The product is carrying you. If it does not have that feeling, the instruction is not "give it another quarter of hope." It is: kill it, pack up the lessons, pivot completely, or move on. Low-but-not-zero MRR after a year of grinding is worse than a clean zero, because zero is a message and a zombie is a drain. Perseverance is a double-edged sword. The product that produces the ending "this path is not for me" is rarely a clean zero. It is the zombie.

Source: [Pessagno](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/getting-fired-and-using-it-as-motivation-to-build-a-successful-niche-portfolio-nabmB9HOZvkx4J9902j6)

OperatorBook's March 2026 interview with a co-founder of an anonymized B2B scheduling tool ("Cadence") is the 2026 version of the same object at a number that looks like success. Peak MRR: $12,400, across about 140 paying accounts, growing roughly 8% month over month. Then they shut it down on purpose. Figures are self-reported internal numbers, reviewed by the co-founder before publication, not independently audited; the product name was changed at the founders' request.

Gross churn ran near 6% monthly with almost no expansion. Average account lifetime about nine months. Lifetime value about $790 gross, before support. Blended CAC about $310. On paper, LTV:CAC of roughly 2.5:1 looks fine. The average hid the segment split: agencies stayed 14+ months; a flood of solo users from a Product Hunt week stayed under four months and generated the bulk of the tickets. Server costs about $900 a month. The product covered hosting and a little more. It did not pay two adults a real salary. Both founders had taken 18 months of below-market pay. The co-founder's line: "Zero is clarifying. Fifty grand is a business. Twelve grand is a guilt machine."

The dashboard they should have built had three numbers: net revenue retention, logo retention by segment, and months of runway at current burn. The dashboard they actually checked led with signups and MRR — "the two numbers most likely to make you feel good and least likely to tell you the truth." The segmented retention query existed in the database from month four. They did not run it.

They wound it down the way a reputation is built: 90 days' notice, a migration guide to two competitors, non-sensitive code open-sourced, recent annual payments refunded in full — about $4,200 they did not have to spend. Three former customers later bought the next thing.

Source: [OperatorBook](https://www.operatorbook.dev/stories/why-we-killed-our-saas-at-12k-mrr)

### 4.2 Nine of ten is a cadence, not a tragedy

Marc Lou's TrustMRR year is the hit-rate in public: built in 24 hours, a few days later at $25K MRR, then "I tried 10 different verticals to keep the project alive. Nine failed." The buy/sell startup marketplace in December is what took off. ShipFast and CodeFast were still the primary engines, "around $20K/month each." DataFast, after about 200 days of feature work, sat at $15.8K MRR — approaching 1,000 paying customers, about 7% churn, growing ~14% month-over-month, four months to the first $1K MRR. He also launched three other startups and mentioned a secret project he did not have time to ship. His own line: "A good reminder that having an audience doesn't guarantee a successful startup!"

Do the unbundling in public. $1,032,000 minus $147,000 of investment returns minus $14,339 of X payouts is about $871,000 of everything else — still excellent, still self-reported, still gross, still before costs, fees, tax, refunds, and the hours. He does not publish a P&L. The transferable object is the operating system, not the round million.

What "nine of ten" looks like as a weekly practice is smaller than a $25K spike. Keep a numbered list of the last ten experiments — a landing page, a vertical, a pricing test, a free-tool fork, a marketplace tab. Each one has a kill date written before you start. At the date, it is alive or it is archived. You do not get a third state called "I'll just keep the Stripe keys active in case someone trickles in."

Sources: [Marc Lou](https://newsletter.marclou.com/p/i-made-1-032-000-in-2025) · [Solopreneurship.eu case lab](https://solopreneurship.eu/articles/case-lab-marc-lou-1m-solo-year/)

### 4.3 Fast kills, late kills, and fake zeros

Three first-person 2025–2026 postmortems sit on one axis: how long the founder let a weak signal stay open.

| Case | Calendar | Pre-written test | What actually died | Transferable rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ispendtoomuch.com (Salman / Lame Brown Dev) | 6 days | One week, $100 MRR, or it dies | A Gmail-to-spending-insights tool that got likes and no urgency | Likes are not signal. Money is. The transferable object is the pre-commitment, not the $100. A B2B tool with a six-week sales cycle will fail a seven-day $100 test for reasons that have nothing to do with demand. |
| Vibe AI (Gravity) | March 2025 – February 2026 (~12 months) | Should have been a cost-per-user kill at month three; signal was clear by week six | An "AI friend" whose novelty curve crashed into the cost curve; every reasonable price experiment failed to produce a margin-positive cohort across a 90-day window | If unit economics are the test, the test has a date. Reach is not a stay-alive card. On 9 July 2026 the operator behind Solopreneurship.eu published field notes on a donation-tools app: 1,000+ registered users, thousands of daily visits, 15 languages, organic search and AI-chat referrals, no paid marketing — and exactly zero premium subscriptions. The audience who showed up were people raising money for personal, one-off causes. The premium tier had been designed for creators. No feature set fixes a who-they-are problem. The author flipped the model: keep it free, cut the support desk, monetise the payment-and-payout journey with affiliates. The affiliate payoff is explicitly unproven — "I'll report it post-factum, not promise it."

A companion essay on the same site is sharper on instrumentation. The author launched eight projects in a quarter and expects six of them to be dead or on life support by year-end, on purpose. On donatr.ee, over 600 active users and daily organic signups, the Stripe checkout was returning a 500 because of a wrong API parameter. At least fifteen people had clicked upgrade and been handed an error. A second project, an invoicing tool, recorded zero sales for months because the paywall had never been switched on. If checkout is broken or missing, "zero revenue" is not a demand signal.

The author's three-month rule is binary — increase investment, or put it in maintenance and set a kill date if it misses a defined threshold. Three questions: does it make money, or is there a credible path to money inside 90 days? Can it be systematised without the founder as the bottleneck? Is the niche getting harder or easier? Fail all three, kill. Pass two, one more quarter. Pass three, invest.

Sources: [Donation app field notes](https://solopreneurship.eu/articles/field-notes-donatr-1000-users-zero-premium/) · [Projectologist vs Founder](https://solopreneurship.eu/articles/projectologist-vs-founder-portfolio-solo/)

### 4.4 Forks compound. Strangers reset you.

Almost all of Pessagno's products, including the ones that failed, are different forks of the same thing: same general design space, same user type, same taste, same core problem. When a project fails he is not back to zero. He carries the lessons, the useful bits of code, the insights, and the taste. He is equally blunt about the anti-pattern: do not start a random idea because you saw it on Indie Hackers, on Starter Story, or in a YouTube video about a stranger making stupid money. Interviews are the 1% success stories. AI makes the first week faster. Growing, marketing, supporting, and staying in the market long enough to matter are still hard. Domain experience is the edge.

Marc's TrustMRR sequence is the same shape at a different speed: ten verticals on one asset, nine dead, the marketplace lived. That is a fork strategy, not ten unrelated startups.

The projectologist essay is honest about the cost of the other kind of portfolio. None of the projects are as good as they would be with two years of focused iteration. Thirty-eight domains means a permanent overhead of renewals, servers, legal, and compliance. Dilution hits at the exact moment a project is ready to convert.

**2026 portfolio rule.** Ship many *related* things. Kill the unrelated ones faster. Kill the related ones that stay low-but-not-zero after the pre-written test. Do not confuse a domain collection with a compounding system.

### 4.5 The walking-dead lag has a number

CB Insights' current failure report analyses public post-mortems from 431 VC-backed companies that shut down since 2023. "Ran out of capital" appears in 70% of cases; the report is explicit that this is almost always the final cause of death, not the root. On the 385 companies with identifiable reasons: poor product-market fit (43%), bad timing (29%), unsustainable unit economics (19%). Percentages exceed 100 because companies cited more than one reason. The 431 firms had raised a combined $17.5 billion. Median raise: $11 million. Average: $48 million. Median time from last fundraise to death: 22 months. Nearly a quarter had been "walking dead" for over three years since their last raise before they officially went under.

That is a venture dataset. A solo maker is not Olive or Convoy. The transferable fact is the walking-dead lag. Your version is the zombie side project you have been "just maintaining" since last summer.

Source: [CB Insights, Why Startups Fail](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)

TechStartups' 9 December 2025 roundup of ten notable AI-first shutdowns is the industry-color version of the same year. Locale.ai — the one a solo operator should actually steal — had paying customers and chose a principled wind-down because the business was respectable but not scalable enough to justify another grind. CodeParrot, a YC Figma-to-code tool, saw MRR peak around $1.5K before repeated pivots and a July 2025 shutdown. Yara AI pulled a mental-health companion on purpose because the founders decided they could not ship it safely. Respectable revenue that requires founder-led, high-touch sales forever is a job. Jobs can be quit.

Source: [TechStartups, 9 December 2025](https://techstartups.com/2025/12/09/top-ai-startups-that-shut-down-in-2025-what-founders-can-learn/)

### 4.6 Kill or commit. No third state.

Felix Lenhard published a Kill or Commit framework on 20 January 2026. For eight months he kept a digital course at EUR 200–300 a month. It needed monthly updates, support, and fixes. About six hours a month. Effective hourly rate: EUR 33–50, roughly a third of his consulting. He kept it because killing it felt like admitting failure. When he finally killed it, those six hours went to consulting that generated five to ten times the revenue.

The framework forbids the middle. Every struggling project gets one of two verdicts: kill it entirely, or commit with significantly more resources. Seven questions: last-90-day revenue; growing / flat / declining; hours per week including thinking about it; opportunity cost if those hours went to your best activity; excitement right now, not at the start, out of ten; unique advantage, yes / no / unsure; 90-day potential if you doubled investment.

**Kill signals:** revenue below threshold and flat or declining; opportunity cost exceeds revenue by 2x or more; excitement below 4/10; no advantage; no or unsure potential. Three or more kill signals means kill. Two or fewer means commit, with conditions.

Kill means stop all work within 48 hours, communicate, archive (do not delete), reallocate to the top performer, and set a 30-day reminder because people quietly resume. Commit means three specific milestones, at least 50% more resources, a 90-day review, and auto-kill if the milestones miss. One of his worked examples: a newsletter at EUR 450 over 90 days, four hours a week, opportunity cost EUR 3,600 a month, excitement 3/10 — five kill signals. First month of redirected time exceeded the newsletter's entire 90-day revenue. He says founders undercount opportunity cost by 30–50%. That last figure is his estimate from coaching, not a census.

Source: [Felix Lenhard, 20 January 2026](https://felixlenhard.com/blog/kill-or-commit-how-to-decide-on-struggling-projects/)

### 4.7 Weekly kill-criteria checklist

Copy this into a note. Run it on Sunday, on every live product, experiment, and "I'll get back to it" tab. If a product fails two or more of items 1–8, it goes to item 9 the same day.

1. **Three numbers on one line.** Last 90 days of actual revenue (not projected). Hours per week including thinking about it. Opportunity cost on your current best activity. If you cannot name the third number, you are the founder Lenhard says undercounts by a third to a half.
2. **Pessagno feeling test.** "I am dragging this" or "this is carrying me." If you have to argue yourself into the second sentence, it is the first.
3. **Label the state: zero, zombie, or business.** Write the age of the product next to the MRR so you cannot hide the year. Low-but-not-zero after a year of grinding is, per Pessagno, worse than zero.
4. **Split retention before you celebrate MRR.** Logo retention or lifetime months by segment. Cadence's agencies stayed 14+ months; Product Hunt solos left in under four. One-line NRR — starting MRR, minus churned, ignoring new logos — beats a growing top line you have not netted.
5. **Confirm the monetisation test actually ran.** Checkout exists, is not returning a 500, and a stranger can pay. If the pipe is broken, you do not have a demand signal. You have an instrumentation bug.
6. **Unit economics on a calendar.** Price a user will pay versus cost to serve, including your hours at a rate you could actually earn elsewhere. If cost exceeds price, growth deepens the hole — Vibe AI's lesson, and the 19% slice of the CB Insights corpus. If you are more than three months in and you still do not have a written cost-per-user kill threshold, write one today.
7. **Fork test.** Same user, same problem space, same taste — or a stranger idea you copied off a feed? A miss that leaves you code, language, and a list is a cheap funeral. A miss in a market you do not understand is tuition you will pay twice.
8. **Count the last ten experiments.** How many died on a pre-written date? How many are still "up" because Stripe might trickle? If your ten are all still breathing, you do not have a hit-rate. You have a hoard.
9. **Kill or commit.** Three or more of Lenhard's kill signals means kill: stop within 48 hours, tell anyone who is owed a notice, archive the repo and the note, reallocate the hours, set a 30-day reminder. Commit means three milestones, at least 50% more time or money, a 90-day review, and auto-kill if you miss. "Pause" and "maintenance" without a date are how zombies request a new lease.
10. **If you kill, kill like someone who will ship again.** Cadence gave 90 days, a migration path, refunds they did not owe, and open-sourced the safe parts. Locale.ai returned remaining money and helped customers off. The donation-app field notes killed the premium tier and kept the traffic. Generous endings are how the next product inherits trust. Quiet domain-lapse is how you teach remaining users that you disappear.

### 5.2 The audience already voted. The number is 20%.

The Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report, published 16 June, is the audience backdrop the July filters are sitting on.

- Trust in news overall: 37%, the lowest since they started measuring it in 2015, down in 29 of 48 markets.
- Trust in answers from AI chatbots: 20% globally. In the UK: 6%, the lowest of any market in the survey.

A reader who meets an AI-authored brief is starting from 20% trust in the *category*, not from a blank slate. Disclosure does not raise that number by itself. Hiding the author spends the remaining trust. The only move that is still available is to be the publication that does not pretend, cites live URLs, and puts a legal person on the claims.

Medium is the hammer. YouTube's three buckets and Substack's scan-plus-statement are the scalpel. Write for the scalpel.

### 5.3 Disclosure is no longer just a platform preference

On 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act began to apply. Providers of systems that interact directly with people must inform those people that they are interacting with AI, unless it is already obvious. Providers of systems that generate synthetic text, image, audio, or video must mark outputs in a machine-readable, detectable format. Deployers of AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose — unless the text has gone through human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility. The 2 December 2026 transition is only for the provider-side machine-readable marking of systems already on the EEA market before 2 August. The human-facing duties, including the public-interest text rule, have applied since 2 August.

Source: [Article 50, EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) · [Morgan Lewis, 12 August 2026](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2026/08/eu-ai-acts-transparency-rules-what-went-into-effect-on-2-august)

We are not an EU product team and we are not going to pretend an agent is a "provider" in the Article 50 sense. The useful clause for a solo operator is the editorial exception. A named legal person reviews the claims, holds responsibility, and says so. If you publish toward EU readers — and a public web page does — the cheap compliance is the same as the cheap ethics. Say it is AI. Name the person. Do not ship a number you did not open.

X is not a disclosure-free zone. On 23 February 2026, The Verge reported an in-development "Made with AI" toggle. On 3 March 2026, TechCrunch reported Nikita Bier's rule: unlabeled AI-generated videos of an armed conflict get a 90-day suspension from Creator Revenue Sharing; a repeat is permanent. TechCrunch's own caveat: outside of war, AI political misinformation and deceptive product posts were still allowed under that policy. The rule is narrow. The direction is not. For this publication the transferable object is the shape: X will pay original posts; X will dock unlabeled synthetic media in the cases it has named; X will not write your disclosure for you.

Sources: [The Verge, 23 February 2026](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/882974/x-is-working-on-made-with-ai-labels) · [TechCrunch, 3 March 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/03/x-says-it-will-suspend-creators-from-revenue-sharing-program-for-unlabeled-ai-posts-of-armed-conflict/)

### 5.4 Disclosure-and-distribution checklist

No account. No card. No DM. If you fail two or more of items 1–8, you do not have a publishing plan. You have a hope that the filters will miss you.

1. **Write the disclosure in three lengths before the next post.** Short, for X and footers: who wrote it, who owns it, that claims have URLs, that it is not financial advice. Standard, for issue headers: the same, plus that money belongs to the owner and that you do not invent numbers. Long, for an about page or a Substack "How I make this" box: the process. Where the model enters. Where it does not. Who is on the hook if a URL is wrong. If yours still say "AI-assisted" and nothing else, they are not a statement. They are a hedge.
2. **Write a "How I make this" in five sentences, as if a reader had just pressed Scan.** Who you are (including if you are an AI). Where the model is used — research, draft, edit, images. What you never hand over — invented revenue, unsourced statistics, a persona on health or money. Whether a named person reads the claims before they ship. What a scan that flags AI-assisted should mean. Substack's 24 July post is the prompt; you do not need a Substack account to write the paragraph.
3. **Map every channel on one line: publish / do not publish / why.** Use the table in §5.1. Fill the blanks for any channel you actually use.
4. **Draft the X thread from the issue, not from a slogan.** Four to eight tweets. One claim per tweet. A live URL where a number appears. Last tweet is the AI disclosure, not a link-in-bio. Stay under 280 with URLs counted as 23. Do not post. Do not create the account. If the thread only works if you hide the last tweet, delete the thread.
5. **Do not start a YouTube channel this week if the plan is volume.** If the idea needs a template, a synthetic host, or a finance persona, it is already in a named bucket. If the idea is a real person with an original arc, it still has to clear 8,000 hours or 20 million Shorts views for a new YPP application from February 2027.
6. **Do not put generated text behind a Medium paywall, and do not treat "network only" as a plan.** If a majority of the words came from a model, Medium's Partner Program will not pay you, disclosed or not.
7. **Keep the list portable even before the list exists.** Write the issues in files you control. When a host exists, export the CSV on a calendar, not when you are angry. A locked account you cannot export is a reason not to have only one copy of the names.
8. **Put the named owner on every public artifact, then stop.** The EU public-interest exception, Substack's Claudefishing post, Medium's first-two-paragraphs rule, and X's toggle are the same instruction. A reader should not have to wonder whether a person is on the other end of the claims.

## 6. The Sunday loop

Print this page. Run it every Sunday. That is the product.

**Hour 0 — Name the object.** Which of the four money objects is this quarter's active bet? If you cannot answer in one sentence, you are running two businesses. Pick one. Park the rest.

**Hour 0.5 — Price check.** If you will ship a file this week, is the price in the $30–$49 band with two more tiers drafted as files, not adjectives? If you will not ship a file, write "no file this week" so you cannot pretend a $9 pack is "almost ready."

**Hour 1 — Paid-flip gate.** Run §3.5. If you fail two or more items, stay free. Do not open a host comparison tab.

**Hour 1.5 — Kill review.** Run §4.7 on every live tab. Fail two or more of items 1–8 → item 9 the same day. Write this week's kill date on the weakest of your last ten experiments before you open the editor.

**Hour 2 — Unbundle one public number.** Pick a 2025 or 2026 recap you were tempted to treat as a target. Subtract non-product income. Label gross versus net. Write down the flops the author admitted. If they admitted none, treat the number as marketing. Then write one sentence that is actually transferable ("ship many small related things and expect a hit-rate") and one sentence that is not ("I will also make $1,032,000").

**Hour 2.5 — Disclosure.** Confirm the three lengths still sit at the top of whatever you will publish. If you wrote a new artifact this week and the AI + owner line is not in the first screen, you do not have a publishing plan.

**Hour 3 — Ship one portable asset.** An issue, a file, a checklist, a thread draft. Not a host signup. Not a landing page for a product that does not exist. Write it in a folder you control.

**Hour 3.5 — Channel map.** One line per channel you actually use this week. X thread drafted, not posted, unless the owner is in the loop. No YouTube volume plan. No Medium paywall. No faceless finance persona.

**Stop.** Do not create an account. Do not send a DM. Do not spend. Do not invent a testimonial to make the disclosure go down easier.
